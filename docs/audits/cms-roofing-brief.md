## What I found

**1. Your Google Ads tracking is wired to a website you don't have anymore.**
Your site loads a Google Ads account (ID 1036689461) with 12 conversion actions and 2 remarketing tags. I read through what each one fires on.
- **Tracked:** phone-number clicks. The "Schedule Now" button is too, but it only appears on your About page.
- **Pointed at things that no longer exist:** at least seven actions are waiting for things that aren't on the current site. That includes a "Make a payment" button, a "Send us a file" button, "Click to schedule service now," "We'd love to hear from you," a /submission-success page, and clicks on jaron@cmsroofing.com (the site now shows office@).
- **Not tracked:** I couldn't find anything that records a completed contact form.
- **Dead tag:** a Universal Analytics tag is still loading, and Google shut that product down in 2024.

If ads are running today, Google is optimizing toward phone clicks only and can't see a single form lead. Whatever the reports say about cost per lead is probably wrong.

**2. The site is slow on a phone, and the tracking is part of why.**
I ran Google's own speed test (Lighthouse) on your homepage in mobile mode. It scored 52 out of 100. The main image and headline took 5.2 seconds to appear, and Google considers anything over 2.5 seconds poor. The page was frozen and unresponsive for about 1.7 seconds while it loaded. Google Tag Manager alone accounted for about 1.25 seconds of that. Cleaning up the dead tags in finding #1 fixes part of this for free.

**3. Leads that come in through the website get no response until someone sees the email.**
Your contact form has its auto-reply switched off, so a homeowner who fills it out at 7pm gets nothing back. The form only asks for name, email, phone, and a message. It doesn't ask for the property address, what they need, or whether there's an insurance claim, so someone has to call back just to find out what the job is. Your listed hours are Monday to Friday, closed weekends. Storm damage doesn't keep those hours, and neither do homeowners comparing three roofers.

There's also a small thing: the email address printed in your website footer has an invisible character hidden in the middle of "cmsroofing." Clicking it works fine. But if someone copies and pastes it into their email, the address will likely fail.

**4. Google sees two different companies.**
Your website and BBB list 308 Old Scottsville Rd. Birdeye, Nextdoor, and the builders association list 384. The website phone is 270-843-5405, and BBB has 270-784-4757. Your start year shows up as 2005 in one place and 2006 in another. Google uses this kind of consistency to decide who shows up in the map results for "roofer near me," and right now it's getting mixed signals. The Birdeye profile hasn't been claimed.

**5. Your reviews are good but not winning.**
You have about 100 Google reviews at roughly 4.2 stars. That's more than Hale (about 14) or Keen (about 49), but they sit at 4.4 and 4.7. The negative reviews mostly mention finish work and warranty follow-through. I couldn't find public replies to them. A calm, specific reply to a bad review does more for the next customer than the review does harm.

**6. There's no financing on the website.**
An unplanned replacement is a big number for most homeowners, and a monthly payment is often what gets it signed this month instead of next year. Best Choice Roofing, the national company with a Bowling Green office, puts financing and same-day inspections up front.

**7. No paid social.**
Nothing is running in the Meta Ad Library under CMS Roofing & Restoration. That means no storm-response ads after hail, no reminders to people who visited the site and didn't book, and no financing message. It also means you haven't wasted money there, so we'd be starting clean.

**8. LinkedIn is talking to the wrong people.**
The company page has 105 followers. The profile image is a cropped logo with white bars, there's no banner, and the last post was homeowner tips about summer heat. Homeowners aren't on LinkedIn. Commercial property owners, facility managers, realtors, insurance agents, and the people you'd want to hire are. That page is also the first place a commercial prospect will look after hearing from you. On top of that, your Facebook logo is a different design from your LinkedIn and website logos.

**9. The commercial side isn't being sold.**
Versico certification plus GAF Master Elite is a real edge for flat and low-slope work, and commercial jobs come in bigger and repeat. Your commercial page explains TPO, EPDM, and modified bitumen in general terms. It doesn't show a single named project, square footage, or before-and-after. The Our Work page is about 200 words of category labels. A facility manager deciding on a $200,000 roof wants to see one that looks like theirs. And nothing goes out and finds that work.

**10. Most of your service area has no page.**
Bowling Green and Owensboro have their own pages. Elizabethtown, Franklin, Glasgow, Portland, Russellville, and Smiths Grove don't. Those are searches you're not showing up for.

## What I can't see yet

The outside view only goes so far. The questions that matter most are inside:

- How many leads come in a month, from where, and how many turn into inspections?
- What's your close rate, and does it change by rep or by lead source?
- How many estimates went out in the last 12 months that never signed, and what are they worth?
- What does a customer cost you by channel, and what is that customer worth over time with repairs, gutters, siding, and referrals?
- How much can your crews actually install between now and spring?

Those answers decide where the first dollar goes. That's why the plan below starts with the numbers.

## What I'd do: 90 days, three phases

### Phase 1: Know the numbers (weeks 1–4)

I'd spend the first weeks in your data and with your team, remotely. That means pulling every lead, estimate, and job, and listening to recorded calls to hear how they get handled. I'd also do a video walk-through with an estimator on a live inspection, and have screen-share sessions with whoever runs the office.

You'd get one report that tells the story of your revenue: where it comes from, where it leaks, and what each lead source is really worth. It covers cost per lead, cost per appointment, cost per customer, gross profit per lead, lifetime value, and how long it takes a lead to turn into cash. You'd also get a live dashboard that keeps those numbers current, and a ranked list of the five biggest leaks with a dollar figure on each.

We'd also agree on how each sale gets credited to a source. Otherwise the dashboard can't be trusted.

### Phase 2: Fix what you already have (weeks 3–8)

We'd work the leak list in order. Based on what I've seen, it probably looks like this:

- **Speed:** every web lead and missed call gets a text within a minute and a call within five. After-hours calls get answered and flagged so an urgent leak doesn't wait until Monday.
- **Follow-up on estimates that didn't sign:** a written sequence with financing and proof, and a simple "where are you at?" message that gets people to answer. This is usually the fastest money in a roofing company, because those people already let you on the roof.
- **Your database:** a check-in to every old lead that never bought, and a yearly check-up offer and referral ask to past customers. You've done thousands of roofs.
- **Cleanup:** one address and phone everywhere, review replies, a better form, financing on the site, and a LinkedIn page aimed at commercial buyers, partners, and hires.
- **AI where it saves real time:** call summaries into the CRM, drafted replies and follow-ups, and lead scoring. A person approves anything a customer sees.

### Phase 3: Build a second lead source and test it (weeks 7–13)

With the basics running, I'd start outbound to commercial property owners, facility managers, and property managers, on email and LinkedIn. It would run as a real test instead of a blast.

There are two offers. **Offer 1** is a full reroof: a free condition report and a written replacement budget. **Offer 2** is a maintenance plan: two inspections a year, priority leak response, and a report after every visit. The question behind the test is whether commercial owners respond more to *reducing risk* than to *a capital project*. I suspect the maintenance plan opens more doors and leads to the reroof later, but the test decides.

Each offer gets tested with five different messages:

| | Clear pitch | Free guide | Video of their roof | Local contractor | Timely reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Full reroof** | 1A | 1B | 1C | 1D | 1E |
| **Maintenance plan** | 2A | 2B | 2C | 2D | 2E |

We'd run the tests one variable at a time and read the results against your current inbound, using the same numbers from Phase 1. Outbound has to earn its budget like anything else. Bowling Green and Owensboro aren't big markets, so every contact counts and nobody gets hit twice.

Your timing is good for this. Commercial owners set next year's budgets in the fourth quarter, and "before next year's budget is set" is a real reason to talk now. Winter is also when residential slows down, which makes it the right time to get the systems running before spring storm season.

## What 90 days can realistically get you

I won't promise a revenue number before I've seen your data. What I can tell you is where results usually show up first.

**Inside 90 days:** revenue recovered from estimates that didn't sign, old leads, and past customers. These people already know you, which is why it's the fastest. You'd also get faster response to new leads, clean numbers you can forecast from, and systems that keep running after the sprint.

**Starting in 90 days, closing over the next several months:** commercial pipeline. That means condition reports done, proposals out, and maintenance plans started. Commercial decisions follow budgets and boards, so the big reroofs usually sign after the first 90 days. The goal for day 90 is a pipeline you can see.
